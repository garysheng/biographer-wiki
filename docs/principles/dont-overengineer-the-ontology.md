# Don't Overengineer the Ontology

There is a near-platonic ontology for capturing a life: [profile, transcripts, characters, moments, timeline, letters](/ontology/the-biographical-ontology). The temptation is to keep going. Settings. Themes. Eras. Relationships-between-characters. Emotion tags. Every one of them feels like rigor and costs you the collection.

The costs are concrete:

- **The agent pays on every cycle.** Each shape is another thing to read, maintain, and keep consistent before a letter can go out. Maintenance work crowds out the actual craft, which is [listening](/concepts/context-collection).
- **Empty categories rot.** A `/settings/` folder with one file teaches every future session that the ontology is aspirational rather than real. Half-followed structure is worse than less structure.
- **Premature categories mis-file the future.** You do not know, in month one, whether this person's story organizes around places, jobs, or people. A thin ontology lets the corpus reveal its own structure; a thick one forces the reveal into last month's guess.

The discipline is the same one that governs code abstraction: **promote on the second instance, never the first.** A person mentioned once lives in the transcript. Mentioned again, they earn a character file. A place that keeps anchoring moments can earn a settings file the day the third moment cites it. The recurrence is the evidence; the file is the response.

And some shapes never earn their way in, because they are [projections](/concepts/forms-are-projections): chapter drafts, theme essays, "best stories" collections. Render those; do not file them.

> **The ontology is discovered by the life being collected, one recurrence at a time. Ship six shapes and let the story argue for the seventh.**
